Model reasoning — Anthropic Claude Opus 4.7

Trump's claim that the One Big Beautiful Bill (signed July 2025) eliminated taxes on Social Security for seniors is technically inaccurate. The law did NOT change the underlying taxation of Social Security benefits; instead, it created a temporary $6,000 'Senior Deduction' (2025-2028) for taxpayers 65+ that phases out above $75,000/$150,000 income (TurboTax, March 2026; Bankers Life, Aug 2025). Per fact-check style sources, the OBBB 'didn't eliminate taxes on Social Security benefits. In fact, it didn't change the way Social Security benefits are taxed at all.' However, the SSA and White House (July 2025) state that the enhanced deduction effectively means about 88-90% of seniors receiving Social Security will pay no federal income tax on their benefits. So the deduction effectively eliminates Social Security taxes for most—but not all—seniors, and only temporarily through 2028. Higher-income seniors and those who already paid no tax see no change, and the underlying taxation framework remains intact. AFSCME (Aug 2025) and AARP analyses note the deduction is roughly one-third the size of an actual elimination of Social Security taxation.

So a claim that the bill “eliminated taxes on Social Security for seniors” overstates what the law did.

📊 Exaggerated
Model reasoning — Google Gemini 2.5 Pro

The "Big Beautiful Bill Act," signed into law in July 2025, did not eliminate the federal income tax on Social Security benefits. Instead, the law created a new, temporary "Senior Bonus Deduction" for tax years 2025 through 2028. This provision allows taxpayers aged 65 and older to take an additional deduction of up to $6,000, which phases out for individuals with modified adjusted gross incomes over $75,000 ($150,000 for joint filers). While this new deduction is substantial enough to reduce the overall tax liability to zero for many lower and middle-income seniors—prompting the Social Security Administration to claim nearly 90% of beneficiaries would no longer pay federal income tax on their benefits—it does not legally eliminate the tax itself. The underlying rules for taxing Social Security benefits remain unchanged, and seniors with higher incomes or those under 65 receiving disability benefits may still owe federal tax on their benefits.
