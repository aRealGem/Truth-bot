Model reasoning — Anthropic Claude Opus 4.7

NATO members did agree at the 2014 Wales Summit to commit 2% of GDP to defense spending, and for years many members fell short—in 2014 only three Allies met the 2% threshold, and as recently as 2023 only a handful of European countries met it. By 2024-2025, however, all 32 NATO members were meeting or exceeding the 2% target. Trump's claim that 'the U.S. paid for almost all of NATO' is exaggerated: while the U.S. is by far the largest spender (around 3.4% of GDP and roughly equal to all other members combined in absolute terms), non-U.S. allies still collectively spend hundreds of billions on defense (over $574 billion in 2025 from European allies and Canada). NATO's common-funded budget is also shared via cost-sharing formulas, not 'paid for almost all' by the U.S.

NATO members agreed to a guideline in 2006, reaffirmed in 2014, to spend 2% of their GDP on defense. For many years, a majority of allies failed to meet this target. However, the claim that the U.S. was "paying for almost all of NATO" is a conflation. The U.S. contributes about 16% of NATO's direct common-funded budget, which is relatively small. The claim's accuracy rests on the fact that the U.S. accounts for the vast majority (over 60%) of the *total combined defense spending* of all member countries, not that it pays other countries' bills or directly funds the bulk of the alliance's operational budget.
