Veterans of the drug war argue interdiction—not strikes that kill crews and destroy intelligence—would be more effective. The DCReport analysis notes the administration claims success but offers no data, no proof, and no transparency tying record seizures to the strike campaign.

The Trump administration did launch an enhanced counter-narcotics military operation on April 1, 2020, through U.S. Southern Command (SOUTHCOM) in the Caribbean and Eastern Pacific. Following this, both the Coast Guard and SOUTHCOM reported large drug seizures, with the Coast Guard noting a record year for cocaine seizures. However, the claim of stopping "record amounts" is misleading because seizure data does not equate to the total flow of drugs, as it's impossible to know how much gets through undetected. Experts note that seizure statistics reflect interdiction activity, not overall trafficking volume, and can fluctuate due to many factors like shifting routes or enforcement strategies. Furthermore, while Customs and Border Protection (CBP) data showed a temporary drop in seizures in late 2025, the U.S. Coast Guard, the primary agency for at-sea interdiction, reported a 200% increase in cocaine seizures during the 2025 fiscal year compared to a typical year. This contradicts the implication that the overall flow of drugs by sea was stopped at record levels.
